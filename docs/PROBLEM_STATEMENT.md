# Problem statement

## The question every entry has to answer

Before a product crosses into the United States or Canada, someone has to say what it is in the language of the
tariff: a ten-digit number in the Harmonized Tariff Schedule of the United States (HTSUS), and a ten-digit
classification in Canada's Customs Tariff. That number decides which duties apply, which trade agreement can be
claimed and which agency rules follow. In both countries the legal responsibility for getting it right sits with
the importer, not the carrier, the marketplace or the software.

For a long time many small importers rarely had to answer it. That changed in 2025.

## What changed

**De minimis ended for everyone.** Since 29 August 2025 the US duty-free de minimis treatment for low-value
shipments (formerly up to $800 per person per day, 19 U.S.C. 1321) has been suspended for goods from every
country (Executive Order 14324). A direct-to-consumer brand that used to ship parcels without formal entries now
files entries with a classification and pays duty on every shipment.

**Duties stack.** On the same customs value, a product can carry its normal (MFN) duty, a Section 301 trade-remedy
duty, Section 232 duties on steel, aluminium or copper content, and one or more emergency duties imposed under the
International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA). They add up; they don't compound. Each has its own Chapter 99
number, effective date, exclusions and stacking rule, and they changed many times during 2025.

As of Thursday 19 February 2026, a 20 oz stainless-steel vacuum tumbler made in China and classified in
9617.00.10.00 carries 7.2% MFN, no Section 301 duty (its line is on the suspended List 4B), a 10% IEEPA duty
linked to synthetic opioids (cut from 20% on 10 November 2025) and a 10% IEEPA reciprocal duty: 27.2% of its value
before fees. Entered as a steel household article of heading 7323, the same tumbler would instead pay 2% MFN plus
the 50% Section 232 duty on its steel content, with the opioid-linked IEEPA duty still on top. One heading decides
whether a $12,500 shipment pays about $3,400 in duty or several thousand dollars more, and CBP itself changed its
mind on this exact question: in 2018 it ruled insulated stainless tumblers into 7323 (NY N297165), and in 2021 it
revoked four such rulings and placed insulated stainless containers in 9617 (HQ H303684).

**The legal ground is moving.** The IEEPA duties were being collected on the demo date while their lawfulness was
before the Supreme Court (*Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump*, argued 5 November 2025, undecided on 19 February
2026). An importer needs to know which part of each landed cost rests on a layer whose status is open, and to keep
the evidence of what they knew and when.

## The legal duties the product is designed to support

**United States**

- **Reasonable care (19 U.S.C. 1484).** The importer of record must use reasonable care to enter, classify and
  determine the value of merchandise. CBP's informed-compliance guidance on reasonable care asks, among other
  things, whether the importer consulted the tariff, the Explanatory Notes and CBP rulings, or sought a ruling or
  expert advice. Failing to do so can lead to penalties under 19 U.S.C. 1592 even where no fraud is involved
  (negligence carries up to the lesser of the domestic value or twice the lost duties).
- **Record keeping (19 CFR Part 163).** Records relating to an entry must be kept for five years from the date of
  entry (19 CFR 163.4). The record has to show the basis for the classification, not only the number.
- **Rulings (19 CFR Part 177).** CBP's binding rulings are the best public evidence of how CBP reads the tariff.
  Applying a ruling to your own goods is "subject to the verification of the facts incorporated in the ruling, a
  comparison of the transaction described therein to the actual transaction" (19 CFR 177.9(b)(1)): comparing facts
  is the actual work.

**Canada**

- **Self-assessment and corrections.** Importers declare the tariff classification and, under section 32.2 of the
  Customs Act, must correct a declaration within 90 days of having reason to believe it was incorrect (CBSA
  Memorandum D11-6-6). Since CARM, importers account for their own duties directly with the CBSA.
- **Records.** Importers must keep the records of commercial imports in Canada for six years (Memorandum
  D17-1-21).
- **Advance rulings.** An importer can ask the CBSA for an advance ruling on tariff classification (Memorandum
  D11-11-3). A product binder with the facts, the evidence and the reasoning is the substance of that request.
- **Classification policy.** The CBSA's D10 memoranda give its policy for particular goods, e.g. parts and
  accessories (D10-0-1) and goods using Bluetooth technology (D10-14-64).

## Who has the problem

- **Small and mid-size importers and e-commerce brands** (the seed company, Northbound Goods Co., sells outdoor
  gear, small electronics, apparel and kitchenware from Vancouver and Seattle, made in China, Vietnam, Mexico and
  the EU). They have hundreds of SKUs, no in-house classification specialist and duty that now lands on every
  shipment.
- **Licensed customs brokers** (the seed firm, Driftwood Customs Brokers) who file their clients' entries and
  answer for the codes. They need a queue of exactly the items that need a professional, each with the evidence
  and the reasoning, and their corrections should improve the next answer instead of disappearing into email.
- **Compliance leads** who must show, years later, what was known at the time and who decided.

## What Tariff Compass does

1. **Classifies** a product described in text, a spec sheet (PDF) or a photo into its US and Canadian ten-digit
   codes, following the General Rules of Interpretation step by step, saying why each neighbouring heading was set
   aside, and citing the CBP rulings that support each sentence. When it isn't sure it says **Needs a broker** and
   asks the one fact that would decide it. It never shows a percentage.
2. **Explains the landed cost**: every duty layer with its rate, amount, plain reason, source, Chapter 99 number
   and effective date, the layers that don't apply and why, and USMCA/CUSMA origin questions where they matter.
3. **Routes to a broker**: unsure answers, answers that differ from the last code, products whose ruling changed and
   items under always-review rules (lithium cells, steel or aluminium content, entries over $25,000) go to the
   broker's docket. Brokers approve, correct with a required reason, or ask the importer. Each correction becomes a
   labelled example for the next training round.
4. **Keeps a binder** for every product: every version of its codes, who decided, why, which rulings and evidence
   it relied on, linked into a tamper-evident activity record; exportable as PDF or ZIP and kept five years after
   the latest US entry, or six after the latest Canadian import when that runs longer.
5. **Watches** the tariff, the duty layers and the rulings, and flags the products each change touches.
6. **Measures itself**: every training round is tested on newer rulings the classifier hasn't seen, against a
   word-matching baseline, and needs two signatures (compliance and a licensed broker) before it goes live.

## Non-goals and limits

- **It files nothing.** A licensed broker approves every code before it is used on an entry; the product prepares
  the evidence, it does not make the declaration.
- **It is not legal advice** and does not replace a binding ruling. It is designed to support reasonable care and
  record keeping; it does not claim compliance or certification.
- **Accuracy is measured, never promised.** The Lab reports accuracy on held-out rulings by depth (chapter,
  heading, subheading, full code) and what each confidence word has meant in practice.
- **Coverage follows the public record.** Rulings exist where importers asked; some headings have few or none.
  Rates and layers are those in force on the data snapshot date and are shown with their sources.
